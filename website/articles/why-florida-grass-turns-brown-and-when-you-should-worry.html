Why Florida Grass Turns Brown - and When You Should Worry

For homeowners on Florida's Treasure Coast, lawn care is shaped by heat, humidity, seasonal rain, sandy soils, warm-season grasses, and a growing season that can feel almost year-round. Why Florida Grass Turns Brown - and When You Should Worry is therefore more than a cosmetic question. The right approach can improve curb appeal while reducing unnecessary stress on the turf and avoiding preventable maintenance problems.

Brown grass is a symptom, not a diagnosis

A brown patch does not automatically mean the lawn needs more water or fertilizer. On the Treasure Coast, similar-looking damage can come from drought stress, excessive moisture, chinch bugs, fungal disease, chemical injury, pet urine, mower scalping, or irrigation coverage problems. Correct identification matters because the wrong treatment can make the problem worse.

Start with irrigation patterns

If brown areas follow sprinkler boundaries, corners, or narrow strips, uneven irrigation may be the cause. Check for broken heads, clogged nozzles, blocked spray patterns, and zones that are running too long or not at all. A screwdriver pushed into the soil can provide a simple comparison of moisture between healthy and stressed areas.

Look closely for insect damage

Chinch bugs are especially associated with St. Augustine grass and can create expanding yellow-to-brown areas, often in hot sunny locations. Other insects can also damage roots or blades. Because pesticide selection and application are regulated and product-specific, suspected infestations are best confirmed before treatment.

Too much water can also cause trouble

Constantly wet turf can encourage disease and weak root systems. Fungal problems often become more noticeable during humid, rainy periods or where irrigation runs unnecessarily on top of natural rainfall. Improving watering practices and mowing conditions may be part of the solution.

Mowing can create brown areas

Dull blades tear grass rather than cutting it cleanly. Cutting too low can expose stems, remove too much leaf tissue, and make high spots appear brown immediately after mowing. Wheel ruts and repeated turning in the same location can also stress wet turf.

When to call for help

If the brown area is expanding, has a distinct pattern, does not respond to corrected irrigation, or appears suddenly after a treatment, professional diagnosis is worthwhile. Take photographs over several days and note watering, rainfall, recent applications, and mowing dates. That history can make diagnosis much easier.

The bottom line

The best decision for a Treasure Coast lawn is the one that fits the grass species, property conditions, season, and homeowner expectations. Florida grass turning brown is most useful as a starting point; consistent mowing practices, observation, and timely correction of irrigation or turf problems are what protect the lawn over the long term.
